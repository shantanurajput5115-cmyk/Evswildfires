The basics

What is a wildfire?

A wildfire is an uncontrolled fire that burns forest, grassland or scrub. Many forest ecosystems burn naturally now and then. The damage comes when fires become more frequent, hotter, or start near people.

Natural causes

Lightning and heat

Lightning strikes, long dry spells and very high temperatures can ignite dry vegetation without any human involvement.

Human causes

Most ignitions

Discarded cigarettes, campfires, crop-residue burning, sparks from power lines and arson start a large share of forest fires.

Climate link

A feedback loop

Warmer, drier weather makes fires easier to start. Fires release CO₂, which adds to warming, which dries forests further.

The fire triangle

Fire needs three things at once: heat, fuel and oxygen. Change each slider and watch the flame. Remove any one side and the fire goes out. This is also why firebreaks and fire-fighting work.

Burning

A fire keeps burning only when all three stay above about 20%. Satellites look for the heat part of this triangle.

Why it matters

Environmental impacts

Wildfire damage spreads far beyond the burnt area.

Biodiversity

Habitat loss

Animals die or flee, seedlings and nests are destroyed, and slow-recovering species may not return.

Air

Smoke and PM2.5

Smoke carries fine particles and carbon monoxide that travel hundreds of kilometres and harm lungs and hearts.

Soil

Erosion

Fire removes the cover that holds soil. Heavy rain afterwards washes ash and soil into streams.

Water

Catchment damage

Burnt slopes absorb less rain, so floods rise faster and drinking water gets muddy and polluted.

Climate

Carbon release

Trees store carbon. Burning releases it as CO₂ and also removes the trees that would absorb more.

People

Livelihoods

Villages that depend on forest produce, tourism and farming lose income, and some must evacuate.

Detection

How satellites spot a fire

Fires are far hotter than the ground around them, so they glow brightly in mid-infrared light. Satellites measure this brightness for every pixel, and an algorithm flags pixels that are unusually hot compared with their neighbours.

1. Satellite passSensor records thermal bands
2. Anomaly scoringPixel vs. neighbours
3. False-alarm filterRemove glint, flares, hot rock
4. GeotagLatitude, longitude, time
5. AlertMap or SMS to forest staff

Case study

Wildfires and satellite alerts in India

Forest fires in India are most common in the dry months before the monsoon. Hill forests with dry pine needles, such as in Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh, burn easily. India's Forest Survey of India sends near-real-time fire alerts to forest officials, using MODIS and VIIRS satellite detections, so field teams can reach a fire early.

Prevention

Fire lines and awareness

Clearing strips of dry litter, controlled early burning, and village fire committees reduce spread.

Response

Alert to action

A geotagged alert gives the forest guard a location, so crews are sent in minutes rather than after smoke is seen.

Goals

SDG 13 and SDG 15

Early detection supports climate action (13) and protecting life on land (15).
